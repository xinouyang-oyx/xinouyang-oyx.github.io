Research Interests

Main Interests

My research focuses on technological innovation and identity-based inequality. I have always thought of the two as closely connected: innovation moves society forward, but it also reshapes who gets ahead. On the innovation side, I study how institutions, incentives, and new technologies affect what gets invented and how new ideas create value. On the inequality side, I study how identity and social institutions shape people's opportunities to participate, advance, and have influence. Much of my work is motivated by what happens when these two forces meet—when technological or institutional change creates new opportunities, but not necessarily in the same way for everyone.

Additional Interests

Beyond these core areas, I am broadly interested in human behavior and broader social patterns. I am especially drawn to ideas that are interesting either because they speak to an unresolved question in the literature or because they emerge from an important real-world phenomenon. I value both kinds of ideas, and my current projects span environmental governance, political and social communication, and urban development.

Thoughts on AI

Having been trained as an empirical researcher just as modern AI began to reshape research, I see AI in two ways. First, I believe it will change the way empirical research is done, and I am rebuilding my own toolkit around an AI-native research workflow while keeping research taste, intellectual depth, and real-world relevance at the center. Second, I study AI itself as one of the defining technological shifts of our time, especially AI for Science—how it changes scientific discovery, expertise, and the organization of innovative work. More broadly, I think economics and management have an important role to play in understanding and designing how AI is organized, governed, and translated into social and organizational value.

Database

I also spend a good deal of time building and improving research datasets. One lesson I have learned is that an existing variable is not necessarily the same thing as a good measure. Administrative records, patents, firm disclosures, and other commonly used data are themselves produced by particular legal, technological, and organizational processes, and those processes often leave their imprint on what we observe. I therefore like to trace measures back to how the underlying data were actually generated, identify where standard measures mix together conceptually different things, and, when possible, build better ones. I have developed and continue to expand large-scale datasets on innovation, labor markets, and economic history, and I am always interested in collaborations that open up new sources of data.
